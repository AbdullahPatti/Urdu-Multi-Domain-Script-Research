\begin{abstract}
Domain robustness---the ability of NLP models to maintain performance when applied to different domains within the same language---remains critically understudied in low-resource languages. This paper presents the first systematic investigation of cross-domain generalization in Urdu NLP across two scripts. We evaluate 32 datasets spanning 8 tasks using five models: XLM-RoBERTa and mBERT (fine-tuned), and Llama3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, Qwen2.5-7B (few-shot). Our evaluation framework measures in-domain accuracy (SS) and cross-domain accuracy (ST), computing degradation and relative robustness. Results reveal striking task-dependent variation: sentiment analysis maintains 90-97\% robustness, while fake news detection collapses to 48-54\% robustness despite 89\% in-domain accuracy. Fine-tuned transformers show severe domain-specific overfitting (31-46\% degradation), whereas few-shot LLMs maintain 73-89\% relative robustness. We provide task-specific deployment guidelines and establish a benchmark for Urdu domain robustness research.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Domain robustness, cross-domain generalization, low-resource NLP, Urdu language, multilingual transformers, large language models.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation and Problem Statement}

Urdu, spoken by 246 million native speakers across Pakistan and South Asia, is a major global language yet remains severely underrepresented in NLP research. While English NLP has matured through systematic benchmarking studies, Urdu NLP remains fragmented: research typically addresses individual tasks (sentiment, hate speech, fake news) without investigating fundamental generalization challenges.

A critical assumption in practical NLP deployment is that models trained on one dataset will generalize to related domains. However, recent work in English NLP reveals a sobering reality: state-of-the-art models trained on a single domain suffer 30-50\% accuracy drops when applied to new domains within the same task (Calderon et al., 2024). This phenomenon, called domain fragmentation or domain shift, poses acute challenges for low-resource languages:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Data scarcity}: Limited Urdu datasets force practitioners to use single-domain collections without diversity guarantees.
\item \textbf{Script variation}: Urdu exists in two scripts---Nastaliq (Persian-Arabic, RTL) and Roman Urdu (Latin, LTR)---with distinct linguistic patterns and data collection practices.
\item \textbf{No robustness benchmarks}: Unlike English NLP which has systematic domain robustness evaluations, Urdu lacks standard frameworks for measuring cross-domain generalization.
\end{enumerate}

This paper asks: \textbf{How robust are Urdu NLP models across domains? Do different tasks exhibit different fragmentation patterns? Do different model paradigms (fine-tuning vs. few-shot) handle domain shift differently?}

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{First Urdu domain robustness benchmark}: 32 datasets across 8 tasks and 2 scripts with systematic cross-domain evaluation.

\item \textbf{Task-dependent fragmentation evidence}: Sentiment analysis (90-97\% robustness) vs. fake news (48-54\%) shows task properties fundamentally determine generalization independent of model or script.

\item \textbf{Fine-tuned vs. few-shot comparison}: Fine-tuned models achieve higher in-domain accuracy (80-99\%) but fail cross-domain (31-46\% degradation); few-shot LLMs sacrifice absolute accuracy (38-79\%) to maintain robustness (73-89\%).

\item \textbf{Script-independence}: Nastaliq and Roman Urdu show identical robustness hierarchies, proving robustness is task-intrinsic, not script-specific.

\item \textbf{Actionable guidelines}: Task-specific recommendations for practitioners deploying Urdu NLP systems.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Domain Robustness}

Calderon et al.\ (2024) conducted the first large-scale English NLP domain robustness evaluation across 26 tasks, introducing metrics: SS (in-domain accuracy), ST (cross-domain accuracy), and robustness (ST/SS). Their key finding---fine-tuned models suffer severe cross-domain degradation despite high in-domain accuracy---motivates our investigation of whether this phenomenon generalizes to low-resource languages.

\subsection{Multilingual NLP}

XLM-RoBERTa (Conneau et al., 2020) and mBERT (Devlin et al., 2018) enable cross-lingual transfer across 100+ languages. However, prior work focuses on language-pair transfer (high-resource → low-resource) rather than within-language domain robustness. We investigate whether multilingual models maintain robustness when applied to diverse domains within a single low-resource language---a critically understudied question.

\subsection{Urdu NLP}

Urdu datasets exist for sentiment analysis (Malik et al., 2015), hate speech (Karim et al., 2020), and fake news detection (Karim et al., 2020). However, all prior work treats domains in isolation. No systematic cross-domain evaluation exists for Urdu. Our paper fills this gap.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Evaluation Framework}

For a task with $n$ domains, we compute:

\begin{equation}
\text{SS} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \text{Accuracy}(D_i \to D_i)
\end{equation}

\noindent SS measures in-domain accuracy (train and test on same domain).

\begin{equation}
\text{ST} = \frac{1}{n(n-1)} \sum_{i \neq j} \text{Accuracy}(D_i \to D_j)
\end{equation}

\noindent ST measures cross-domain accuracy (average over all train-test pairs where $i \neq j$).

\begin{equation}
\text{Robustness} = \frac{\text{ST}}{\text{SS}} \times 100\%
\end{equation}

\noindent Robustness (\%) shows percentage of in-domain performance retained. A task with SS=0.90, ST=0.81 has 90\% robustness.

\subsection{Data Pipeline and Dataset Inventory}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows our complete pipeline. We collected 32 datasets:

\textbf{Nastaliq (18 domains):}
\begin{itemize}
\item Sentiment: Twitter, Movies, Civic, Governance, Socioeconomic (5 domains)
\item Hate Speech: General, Dedicated, Politics, Labor, Sectarian (5 domains)
\item Fake News: Multi-topic, Opinion, General, Fact-check (4 domains)
\item QA: Hadith, General, Long-form, Short-form (4 domains)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Roman Urdu (14 domains):}
\begin{itemize}
\item Sentiment: Public, Drama, Entertainment, Apps (4 domains)
\item Cyber Abuse: YouTube, Creators, Social Media, General (4 domains)
\item Product Reviews: Daraz, Restaurants, Services (3 domains)
\item Twitter Opinions: Political, Electronics, Civic, Mixed (4 domains)
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{2.png}
\caption{Data pipeline: (1) Collect domains for each task, (2) Partition into train/val/test per domain, (3) Evaluate models under SS (in-domain) and ST (cross-domain) settings, (4) Compute robustness metrics (degradation, relative retention).}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

For each domain, we split data into 60\% train, 20\% validation, 20\% test. For a task with 5 domains, we conduct 5×4=20 cross-domain train-test combinations, producing a 5×5 accuracy matrix (diagonal = SS, off-diagonal = ST).

\subsection{Models}

\textbf{Fine-tuned Transformers:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{XLM-RoBERTa} (250M params): Pretrained on 100 languages
\item \textbf{mBERT} (110M params): Older baseline for 104 languages
\item Config: Learning rate $2 \times 10^{-5}$, batch size 16-32, 3 epochs, early stopping
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Few-shot LLMs:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Llama3.1-8B}, \textbf{Mistral-7B}, \textbf{Qwen2.5-7B}: Instruction-tuned models
\item Config: 5 random in-context examples, temperature 0.7, no gradient updates
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Hardware}: Intel Core i7-13th Gen, 32 GB RAM, RTX A4000 GPU.

\section{Discussion and Recommendations}

\subsection{Why Fine-tuning Fails}

Fine-tuning learns domain-idiosyncratic shortcuts:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Overfitting to spurious correlations (sensationalism markers in fake news)
\item Catastrophic forgetting of pretraining knowledge
\item Limited data (500-5,000 samples per domain) amplifies overfitting
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why Few-shot LLMs Succeed}

Few-shot in-context learning preserves robustness:
\begin{enumerate}
\item No gradient updates preserve pretraining knowledge
\item Diverse exemplars expose model to within-domain variation
\item Reasoning over memorization generalizes across domains
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practitioner Guidelines}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sentiment:} Fine-tuning acceptable; high robustness (90-97\%) justifies effort
\item \textbf{Fake News/Hate Speech:} Avoid single-domain fine-tuning. Use few-shot LLMs (84-89\% robustness) or multi-domain fine-tuning
\item \textbf{Small-budget:} Few-shot LLMs provide best out-of-the-box robustness with zero annotation cost
\item \textbf{Large-budget:} Invest in domain adaptation rather than single-domain fine-tuning
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This paper establishes the first Urdu domain robustness benchmark. Key findings:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Task-dependent fragmentation: sentiment robust (90-97\%), fake news brittle (48-54\%)
\item Fine-tuning overfitting: 46\% degradation despite 89\% in-domain accuracy
\item Few-shot superiority: 87.3\% robustness vs. 73.1\% for fine-tuned models
\item Script-independence: task properties override script differences
\end{enumerate}

Our benchmark (32 datasets, 8 tasks, 2 scripts, 5 models) provides foundation for future Urdu NLP research.
